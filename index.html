Introduction

Intelligence is not just another software revolution.

The evolution of AI is often measured through model rankings, benchmarks, parameter counts and the cost of GPUs. Those metrics are useful but incomplete. A deeper transformation is underway: AI is changing the architecture of computing, the economics of software, the relationship between people and machines, and perhaps the way science itself advances.

I see this history unfolding in four overlapping ages. Each age makes something previously difficult more accessible, while exposing a new constraint. Each also asks a different question about what remains uniquely valuable in human activity.

The boundary between human intelligence and machine capability is continuously being renegotiated.
Age 01 · Before 2017

The Age of Alchemy

The first age was defined by architectural experimentation: different neural networks for different problems.

Convolutional neural networks transformed computer vision by exploiting spatial locality. RNNs and LSTMs modeled sequences through recurrent state. Encoder–decoder systems supported translation. GANs, autoencoders, reinforcement learning and autoregressive models explored other learning objectives and problem structures. Each technique embodied an assumption about the world; no single architecture offered a reusable foundation across a broad range of tasks.

I call it the Age of Alchemy not because the work lacked mathematics, but because progress often depended on assembling architectures, training objectives and optimizers through empirical experimentation. Insights were transferable, yet implementation and training remained fragmented.

The Transformer breakthrough

The 2017 publication of Attention Is All You Need marked a critical inflection point. Self-attention allowed a token representation to depend dynamically on relationships with other tokens, without requiring recurrent hidden-state updates across sequence positions during training. The architecture proved unusually compatible with massively parallel accelerators.

Transformers did not invent attention, eliminate specialized networks, or solve every learning task. But they helped create a common foundation around which models, tooling, research and computational investment could converge.

The first transition was from architectural fragmentation toward architectural convergence.

The human question: imitation versus understanding

These architectures reproduced abilities once associated with human intelligence: recognition, perception, language and prediction. Their success raised a question that persists today: does reproducing intelligent behavior imply understanding, or merely a useful computational approximation? Increasing performance does not settle that philosophical distinction.

Age 02 · 2017 to present

The Age of Large Language Models

The second age is the convergence of scalable learning architectures with massively parallel computation and enormous datasets.

The original Transformer was encoder–decoder. Encoder-only, encoder–decoder and decoder-only models developed along distinct paths. Decoder-only autoregressive models became especially influential for open-ended generation because next-token prediction provided a relatively uniform objective that scaled across vast bodies of language and code.

As pretraining, post-training, model capacity and dataset quality improved, general-purpose models could handle an expanding range of tasks through zero-shot and few-shot instructions. ChatGPT made this capability broadly accessible through everyday language.

Parallelism was as important as modeling

The revolution was not merely the addition of attention. It also depended on a change in computational organization. A recurrent network updates sequential hidden states: the next state depends on the previous one. That dependency restricts parallel computation along the sequence. Transformers make much of training and prompt processing expressible as large matrix and tensor operations across many token positions at once.

This is a shift toward block-oriented parallel computation. Parallel computation, memory tiling and block updates were not invented by Transformers. Rather, Transformers made these patterns central to general-purpose language learning at scale. GPUs and tensor accelerators could execute many operations together, while batch processing and optimized kernels improved utilization.

The memory movement problem

FLOPs alone do not determine performance. Data transfers between global high-bandwidth memory, caches and registers can dominate time and energy. Tiled matrix multiplication, kernel fusion, reduced precision and techniques such as FlashAttention reduce unnecessary memory movement by operating on blocks with improved local reuse.

The mathematical operation can remain equivalent while its execution becomes dramatically more efficient. This is an architectural insight that joins algorithms and semiconductor design: where computation is scheduled and how data moves can matter as much as the number of operations.

One bottleneck remains. Autoregressive generation still produces unknown output tokens sequentially. Training and prompt ingestion parallelize much more readily than ordinary token-by-token decoding. Consequently, KV-cache management, memory bandwidth, batching, speculative decoding, latency and energy remain central inference challenges.

From tokens to trajectories

Reasoning methods, reinforcement learning and tool use increasingly transform a single model response into a sequence of problem decomposition, external retrieval, actions, verification and revision. More compute can help explore possible solutions, but a longer trajectory does not guarantee correctness: early mistakes can compound and tool outputs may be untrustworthy.

The bottleneck therefore begins shifting from raw model capability toward reliable execution and validated outcomes.

Physical and economic constraints

Training and serving highly capable models requires power generation and distribution, cooling, memory bandwidth, network fabrics, advanced semiconductors and vast capital investment. Scaling faces real constraints in physical infrastructure.

Deployment also encounters a separate limitation. Enterprises contain legacy workflows, undocumented exceptions, governance requirements and sensitive data. Benchmark scores do not directly establish business value. The relevant question is whether intelligence can execute useful work reliably and economically.

The human question: authenticity and judgment

When machines can generate persuasive language and analysis, authorship becomes harder to establish. More deeply, convenient answers can encourage people to outsource evaluation itself. AI may democratize knowledge while weakening the habit of checking assumptions. Greater machine intelligence does not automatically lead to better human judgment.

The second age scaled general-purpose intelligence — and exposed the cost of compute, inference and trust.
Age 03 · Emerging now

The Age of Agentic Computing

The third age is not merely about autonomous agents. It is a restructuring of computing around intentions, persistent context and dependable action.

Conventional software is application-centric: people navigate interfaces and adapt their work to predefined workflows. Agentic computing changes the interaction. A user specifies an objective and a system selects resources, plans operations, invokes tools and evaluates results. Applications become capabilities agents can call, rather than interfaces humans must always operate directly.

The reinvention of personal computing

AI workloads emphasize large model weights, memory bandwidth, attention caches and persistent contextual state. CPUs, GPUs and dedicated accelerators may become more tightly coupled through heterogeneous architectures and unified memory. This is not necessarily the literal merger of CPUs and GPUs; different processors still serve different computational roles.

Operating systems may need to manage semantic retrieval, persistent agent memory, task continuation, model scheduling and permissions alongside conventional processes and files. Personal computers could increasingly support continuous intelligence rather than only the execution of individual applications.

Local and cloud agents coexist

Local models offer privacy, latency and offline availability; cloud models offer access to larger-scale computation. An efficient personal agent may route different tasks among on-device, edge and cloud resources. The result is a distributed intelligence environment in which models, tools, storage and permissions cross several trust boundaries.

AI-generated software challenges SaaS

Vibe coding signals a structural reduction in the effort required to turn specifications into working code. As agents become capable of designing, testing and maintaining systems, software may increasingly be generated around a company's workflow rather than forcing workflows into a predefined application.

This challenges seat-based pricing and traditional SaaS interfaces, but it does not make enterprise software disappear. Authoritative data, transaction networks, compliance, integration, reliability and accountability remain difficult and valuable. Code becomes cheaper; dependable systems remain hard.

Security, networking and information abundance

Autonomous execution requires task-scoped credentials, explicit authorization, audit trails, isolation, idempotency, recovery from partial failure and provenance across agent-to-agent communication. Untrusted emails, web pages and tool outputs must remain data, not commands with inherited authority.

Meanwhile, machine-generated code, documents, reports and messages may proliferate faster than human attention. Information becomes abundant, while verification and trust become scarce.

The human question: agency and purpose

When agents can recommend and perform decisions, the boundary between assistance and surrender becomes consequential. Who sets the objective? Who has the right to approve irreversible actions? How can people retain judgment when delegation becomes effortless?

Work also provides meaning, status and community, not just income. If economic productivity becomes less dependent on human cognitive labor, institutions may need to redefine participation and opportunity. Human worth cannot be inferred from how economically competitive someone is with a machine.

The key scarcity of agentic computing may be trusted execution, accountable control and meaningful human agency.
Age 04 · A twenty-year hypothesis

The Age of Scientific Renaissance

The fourth age may reverse the relationship between scientific knowledge and AI: instead of only using physics and mathematics to build better machines, intelligent machines may help generate new mathematics and physics.

From applied mathematics to new abstractions

Models already help search for patterns, optimize algorithms and assist with theorem proving. Formal proof systems can verify that a proposed proof follows from a given logical foundation. The more ambitious possibility is that AI assists in inventing new abstractions — mathematical concepts that allow researchers to ask questions they previously could not formulate.

The history of calculus, geometry and abstract algebra illustrates how new mathematical languages reshape science and engineering. Automated proof search alone is not equivalent to inventing a conceptual framework, but together with human mathematical insight it may expand the frontier.

From prediction to physical discovery

Physics is harder because mathematical coherence does not establish physical truth. Competing theories may fit the same measurements. AI-enabled science must connect hypotheses to discriminating experiments, observations and independent replication. Automated laboratories could integrate theory proposals, experimental design, data acquisition and model revision into faster feedback cycles.

This might accelerate materials science, chemistry, energy research and complex systems. New fundamental physics, including an advance comparable to relativity or quantum mechanics, is possible but far from guaranteed. Sometimes experiments, not computation, are the limiting resource.

Quantum computing and new substrates

Fault-tolerant quantum computing could eventually contribute to selected simulations of quantum systems, chemistry and materials. It is not a general replacement for classical computation. Photonic, neuromorphic and other heterogeneous physical architectures may also change how machine intelligence is implemented.

AI could contribute to quantum control, materials discovery and hardware design, while advances in physical technology could improve the capacity and efficiency of AI. That would create a reinforcing loop, but its progress will remain constrained by physical feasibility and empirical evidence.

More capable AI→New science→New technology→Better computation

The human question: understanding and purpose

Suppose machines become significant contributors to discoveries beyond the reach of individual human researchers. Humanity could gain extraordinary scientific power while struggling to comprehend or govern some of its consequences. Yet scientific capability is not moral authority. Knowing what can be built does not decide what should be built or who should benefit.

Human responsibility may shift toward selecting questions, interpreting evidence, deciding acceptable risks and governing how technologies are used.

The deepest outcome may not be automated work, but an expansion of humanity's capacity to discover — paired with a greater responsibility to choose wisely.
Synthesis

What becomes scarce when intelligence becomes abundant?

In the first age, reusable learning architectures were scarce. In the second, general-purpose model capability expanded, while computation, energy and practical reliability became constraining. In the third, software generation and digital execution may become cheap, making trust, accountability and human agency more important. In the fourth, exploratory reasoning itself may accelerate, while experimental truth and human judgment remain indispensable.

The four ages are not a deterministic historical law. They overlap, and future architectures may depart radically from today's Transformers and GPU-centered infrastructure. The third age is emerging; the fourth is a hypothesis, not a forecast with a guaranteed deadline.

Nor does increasing machine capability imply inevitable human irrelevance. People do not derive all value from labor-market productivity or intellectual dominance. Creativity, relationships, responsibility, curiosity and meaning cannot be reduced to benchmark scores.

Age 1 asked how to make machines learn. Age 2 asked how far machine intelligence could scale. Age 3 asks how society should operate when intelligence becomes pervasive. Age 4 may ask how humanity defines its purpose when machines become partners in scientific discovery.

Perhaps the most important consequence of building intelligent machines is that we are forced to think more deeply about what it means to be human.
